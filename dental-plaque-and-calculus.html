Dental Plaque & Calculus

Dental plaque is a structured microbial biofilm that develops on tooth surfaces. When plaque becomes mineralized, it forms dental calculus, commonly called tartar.

Anatomy of the Tooth–Gingiva Interface
Plaque commonly accumulates near the gingival margin, where inadequate plaque control may contribute to gingival inflammation.
Key concept: Dental plaque is a living microbial biofilm. Calculus is not simply "old plaque"; it is mineralized dental plaque containing inorganic components derived largely from saliva or gingival crevicular fluid.

What Is Dental Plaque?

Dental plaque is a structured microbial biofilm that develops on teeth and other oral surfaces. It is embedded in an extracellular matrix and contains microorganisms, salivary components, host proteins and other organic and inorganic substances.

Plaque accumulation at the gingival margin can stimulate an inflammatory response in the gingival tissues. If plaque-associated inflammation persists and progresses in susceptible individuals, periodontal tissues may become involved.

Dental Plaque as a Structured Biofilm
Plaque contains microorganisms organized within an extracellular matrix attached to the acquired pellicle on the tooth surface.

How Does Dental Plaque Form?

1. Acquired pellicle

Salivary proteins and glycoproteins rapidly form a thin conditioning film over the clean tooth surface.

2. Initial attachment

Early colonizing microorganisms attach to receptors within the acquired pellicle.

3. Biofilm growth

Additional microorganisms attach and the microbial community becomes increasingly organized.

4. Mature plaque

A complex biofilm develops within an extracellular matrix containing microbial and host-derived components.

What Is Dental Calculus?

Dental calculus is mineralized dental plaque. Mineralization can occur when calcium phosphate salts become incorporated into the plaque biofilm.

Calculus provides a rough surface that can facilitate further plaque retention. However, the inflammatory response is primarily associated with the microbial biofilm on and around the calculus, rather than calculus itself acting as an independent infectious agent.

From Plaque to Mineralized Calculus
Plaque may become mineralized over time, producing a hard calculus deposit that cannot be removed effectively by ordinary brushing alone.

Types of Dental Calculus

Feature Supragingival Calculus Subgingival Calculus
Location Coronal to the gingival margin Apical to the gingival margin within the gingival sulcus or periodontal pocket
Common appearance Usually white, cream or yellowish Usually darker because of incorporation of blood and gingival crevicular components
Common sites Near major salivary gland duct openings, especially mandibular lingual and maxillary buccal surfaces Root surfaces within periodontal pockets
Consistency Often relatively chalky or moderately hard Often dense and firmly attached to the root surface
Detection Usually visible clinically Often requires periodontal probing and tactile detection; radiographs may reveal larger deposits
Supragingival vs Subgingival Calculus
The position of calculus relative to the gingival margin is the main basis for classifying it as supragingival or subgingival.

Why Does Plaque Cause Gingival Inflammation?

When plaque accumulates around the gingival margin, bacterial products and host–microbial interactions can trigger an inflammatory response in the gingival tissues.

1

Plaque accumulation

Microbial biofilm accumulates around the gingival margin, especially when plaque removal is inadequate.

2

Host response

The gingival tissues respond to microbial challenge through inflammatory and immune mechanisms.

3

Clinical gingivitis

Redness, edema and bleeding on probing may develop as inflammation becomes clinically apparent.

Plaque, Calculus and Periodontal Disease

Important periodontal concept: Calculus is an important local plaque-retentive factor, but periodontal destruction is a host–microbial process. The presence of calculus alone does not determine the severity of periodontal disease.
  • Plaque accumulation can initiate gingival inflammation.
  • Calculus provides an irregular surface that favors plaque retention.
  • Subgingival deposits can complicate effective plaque control.
  • Persistent inflammation may contribute to periodontal tissue breakdown in susceptible individuals.
  • Professional periodontal instrumentation is often required to remove calculus deposits.

How Can Plaque and Calculus Be Prevented?

01

Toothbrushing

Brush systematically twice daily using an appropriate fluoride toothpaste and technique.

02

Interdental cleaning

Clean between teeth using floss, interdental brushes or another method appropriate to the individual.

03

Professional care

Dental examination and professional scaling can remove calculus that cannot be eliminated through routine brushing.

BDS Quick Revision

Dental Plaque

  • Structured microbial biofilm attached to oral surfaces.
  • Contains microorganisms within an extracellular matrix.
  • Develops on the acquired pellicle.
  • Plaque at the gingival margin is strongly associated with gingival inflammation.

Dental Calculus

  • Mineralized dental plaque.
  • Classified mainly as supragingival or subgingival according to its location.
  • Supragingival calculus is commonly associated with salivary mineral sources.
  • Subgingival calculus is associated with components of gingival crevicular fluid and blood.
  • Calculus acts as a plaque-retentive local factor.

One-line exam answer

Dental plaque is a structured microbial biofilm, whereas dental calculus is mineralized dental plaque firmly attached to the tooth surface.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is dental plaque the same as calculus?

No. Plaque is a soft microbial biofilm, whereas calculus is mineralized plaque.

Can brushing remove calculus?

Brushing is highly important for plaque control, but established calculus usually requires professional mechanical removal.

What is tartar?

Tartar is the common term used for dental calculus.

What is the difference between supragingival and subgingival calculus?

Supragingival calculus is located above the gingival margin, while subgingival calculus is located below the gingival margin, commonly on root surfaces within periodontal pockets.

Does calculus cause gum disease?

Calculus contributes to plaque retention and makes plaque control more difficult. The inflammatory response to the microbial biofilm is central to periodontal disease development.
