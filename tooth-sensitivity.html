Why Are My Teeth Sensitive?

Tooth sensitivity is a common dental complaint. A particularly important cause is dentinal hypersensitivity, in which exposed dentin responds with a short, sharp pain to normally non-painful stimuli.

Quick definition: Dentinal hypersensitivity is classically described as a short, sharp pain arising from exposed dentin in response to thermal, evaporative, tactile, osmotic or chemical stimuli, when the pain cannot be explained by another dental defect or disease.

However, not every sensitive tooth has dentinal hypersensitivity. Caries, cracked teeth, defective restorations, pulpal inflammation, periodontal conditions, tooth wear and other conditions can produce similar symptoms. Therefore, correct diagnosis comes before treatment.

1. What Happens Inside a Sensitive Tooth?

Dentin lies beneath enamel in the crown and beneath cementum in the root. It contains microscopic channels called dentinal tubules. When protective tooth structure is lost and dentin becomes exposed, external stimuli can influence the fluid within these tubules.

2. The Hydrodynamic Concept

One of the most widely accepted explanations for dentinal hypersensitivity is the hydrodynamic mechanism. External stimuli can cause rapid movement of fluid within exposed dentinal tubules. This movement is associated with activation of sensory structures near the pulp-dentin complex, producing the characteristic sharp pain.

Simplified illustration of stimulus-associated fluid movement within dentinal tubules.

Exam point: The hydrodynamic concept is an important explanation for the sharp, stimulus-dependent pain associated with exposed sensitive dentin.

3. Why Does Dentin Become Exposed?

Gingival recession

Loss or apical displacement of gingival tissue can expose the cervical root surface and underlying dentin.

Tooth wear

Abrasion, erosion, attrition and combined tooth-wear processes can remove protective tooth structure.

Cervical lesions

Non-carious cervical tooth structure loss can leave dentin exposed to thermal and tactile stimuli.

Periodontal therapy

Root-surface exposure following periodontal treatment may temporarily increase sensitivity in some patients.

Acid erosion

Dietary or intrinsic acids can soften dental hard tissues and contribute to loss of protective structure.

Whitening

Some tooth-whitening procedures can temporarily increase tooth sensitivity.

4. Is It Really Dentinal Hypersensitivity?

This is one of the most important clinical questions. Dentinal hypersensitivity should not simply be diagnosed whenever a patient says “my tooth is sensitive.”

Condition Typical clue
Dentinal hypersensitivity Short, sharp pain triggered by a stimulus and generally stops shortly after the stimulus is removed.
Dental caries Sensitivity may occur with a carious lesion and can progress as pulpal involvement develops.
Reversible pulpitis Stimulus-dependent pain with relatively brief duration after removal of the stimulus.
Irreversible pulpitis Spontaneous or lingering pain, thermal pain that persists, and other pulpal symptoms require careful evaluation.
Cracked tooth Pain may be associated with biting or release of biting and can mimic sensitivity.
Defective restoration A recent or defective restoration can produce postoperative sensitivity or symptoms requiring pulpal assessment.
Periodontal/root exposure Recession and exposed root surfaces can produce cervical sensitivity.
Clinical caution: Persistent, spontaneous, severe, nocturnal or lingering pain should not automatically be labelled as dentinal hypersensitivity. A proper endodontic and restorative assessment may be necessary.

5. Clinical Assessment

A structured examination helps determine whether the complaint is consistent with dentinal hypersensitivity or another dental condition.

  1. History: Ask about onset, location, provoking stimulus, duration, severity and whether the pain lingers.
  2. Visual examination: Look for caries, cracks, restorations, erosion, abrasion, recession and exposed root surfaces.
  3. Periodontal assessment: Evaluate gingival recession, plaque, inflammation and periodontal status where appropriate.
  4. Sensitivity testing: Controlled thermal or tactile stimuli may help reproduce the patient's characteristic pain.
  5. Pulpal assessment: When symptoms suggest pulpal disease, appropriate pulp sensibility testing should be considered.
  6. Radiographic examination: Radiographs are selected when the clinical findings indicate a need for further investigation.
Key principle: Diagnosis of dentinal hypersensitivity is largely based on the clinical presentation and exclusion of other causes of similar pain.

6. Management of Dentinal Hypersensitivity

Management should begin by identifying and controlling the cause of dentin exposure or the factor aggravating the sensitivity.

1. Remove the cause

Address contributing factors such as traumatic brushing, erosive dietary habits, plaque-related inflammation or other identifiable causes.

2. Home desensitization

Desensitizing toothpastes may be recommended as an initial non-invasive approach depending on the clinical situation.

3. Fluoride-based approaches

Professionally applied fluoride preparations may be considered to help reduce sensitivity and protect exposed tooth surfaces.

4. Occlusion or restoration

Selected cervical defects or exposed areas may require restorative management when indicated.

5. Periodontal management

When recession or periodontal disease contributes to root exposure, periodontal management may be necessary.

6. Endodontic treatment

Root canal treatment is not a routine treatment for ordinary dentinal hypersensitivity. It may become appropriate only when the actual diagnosis indicates pulpal disease or in carefully selected persistent cases after appropriate assessment.

8. How Can Tooth Sensitivity Be Prevented?

  • Use a gentle brushing technique and avoid excessive brushing force.
  • Use appropriate fluoride toothpaste according to individual needs.
  • Limit frequent exposure to acidic foods and beverages.
  • Do not immediately brush aggressively after significant acidic exposure.
  • Maintain good plaque control.
  • Address gingival recession and periodontal problems when present.
  • Discuss tooth-whitening sensitivity with a dentist before and during whitening treatment.
  • Seek dental evaluation when sensitivity is persistent or unusual.

9. BDS Quick Revision

Definition:
Short, sharp pain from exposed dentin produced by stimuli and not explained by another dental pathology.

Important mechanism:
Hydrodynamic movement of fluid within dentinal tubules.

Important causes:
Gingival recession, non-carious tooth wear, erosion, abrasion, periodontal therapy and other causes of dentin exposure.

Important clinical rule:
Exclude caries, cracked tooth, defective restoration and pulpal/endodontic disease before diagnosing dentinal hypersensitivity.

10. Frequently Asked Questions

Why do my teeth hurt when I drink something cold?

Cold can stimulate exposed dentin and produce a short, sharp response. However, cold sensitivity can also occur with other dental conditions, so persistent or unusual symptoms should be evaluated clinically.

Is tooth sensitivity always dentinal hypersensitivity?

No. Caries, cracks, defective restorations, pulpal disease, periodontal conditions and exposed root surfaces can produce similar symptoms. Dentinal hypersensitivity is diagnosed after other causes have been considered.

Can sensitive teeth be treated?

Yes. Management depends on the cause. Options can include controlling causative factors, desensitizing products, professional fluoride treatment, restorative or periodontal treatment, and treatment of underlying pulpal disease when present.

Does sensitive teeth always mean I need a root canal?

No. Ordinary dentinal hypersensitivity does not automatically require root canal treatment. Endodontic treatment is considered when the clinical diagnosis indicates pulpal disease or another appropriate indication.
