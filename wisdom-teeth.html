Wisdom Teeth (Third Molars)

A comprehensive dental guide to third molars, eruption, impacted wisdom teeth, classification of impaction, pericoronitis, diagnosis, extraction indications, complications and clinical management.

What Are Wisdom Teeth?

Wisdom teeth are the third molars located at the most posterior part of the upper and lower dental arches. They are the last permanent teeth to develop and erupt.

Third molars commonly erupt during late adolescence or early adulthood, although the timing varies considerably between individuals. Some third molars erupt into a functional position, while others remain partially or completely unerupted.

Key concept: A third molar is considered impacted when it cannot erupt into a normal, functional position because of obstruction, insufficient space, abnormal angulation or another mechanical/developmental factor.

Development and Eruption of Third Molars

Third molars develop later than the other permanent teeth. Their development and eruption are influenced by jaw growth, available arch space, tooth angulation and the position of surrounding structures.

Upper Third Molars

Maxillary third molars are located posterior to the second molars. Their eruption path may be affected by the maxillary tuberosity, adjacent second molar and available posterior space.

Lower Third Molars

Mandibular third molars are particularly prone to impaction because of limited space between the second molar and the anterior border of the ramus.

Important: Eruption age should not be used alone to determine whether a third molar requires removal. Clinical and radiographic findings are more important.

Impacted Wisdom Teeth

An impacted third molar is unable to erupt completely into a normal functional position within the dental arch.

Common Causes of Impaction

  • Insufficient space in the dental arch
  • Abnormal tooth angulation
  • Abnormal eruption path
  • Position of the mandibular ramus
  • Obstruction by the adjacent second molar
  • Dense surrounding bone
  • Delayed or altered eruption
  • Developmental variations
Impaction is especially common with mandibular third molars because available posterior space may be insufficient for complete eruption.

Winter's Classification of Impacted Third Molars

Winter's classification describes the position of an impacted third molar according to its long axis in relation to the long axis of the adjacent second molar.

Type Description Clinical significance
Mesioangular Crown is tilted toward the second molar. Common mandibular impaction pattern.
Distoangular Crown is tilted away from the second molar. May present greater surgical difficulty depending on depth and anatomy.
Vertical Long axis is approximately parallel to the second molar. Eruption may be possible if sufficient space is available.
Horizontal Tooth is positioned approximately perpendicular to the second molar. Usually requires careful surgical assessment.

Pell and Gregory Classification

Pell and Gregory classification is mainly used for mandibular third molars. It evaluates the relationship of the tooth to the mandibular ramus and its depth relative to the occlusal plane of the adjacent second molar.

Relationship to the Anterior Border of the Ramus

Class Description
Class I Sufficient space between the distal surface of the second molar and the anterior border of the ramus for the mesiodistal width of the third molar.
Class II Space is less than the mesiodistal width of the third molar, so part of the tooth is within the ramus.
Class III Most or all of the third molar is positioned within the ramus.

Depth of the Impacted Tooth

Position Description
Position A Occlusal surface of the third molar is approximately at the level of the occlusal plane of the second molar.
Position B Occlusal surface lies between the occlusal plane and the cervical line of the second molar.
Position C Occlusal surface lies below the cervical line of the second molar.
BDS memory tip: Pell & Gregory = Ramus relationship + Depth.

Symptoms and Problems Associated With Wisdom Teeth

Pain

Pain may result from pericoronitis, caries, periodontal inflammation, pressure or other local pathology.

Swelling

Local gingival or facial swelling can occur when inflammation or infection develops around an impacted tooth.

Food Trapping

A partially erupted tooth may create an area where food and plaque accumulate around the operculum.

Limited Mouth Opening

Trismus may occur when inflammation extends into surrounding tissues.

Dental Caries

Partially erupted or difficult-to-clean third molars may develop caries, and adjacent second molars may also be affected.

Periodontal Problems

Plaque accumulation around an impacted third molar can contribute to periodontal inflammation and defects.

Pericoronitis

Pericoronitis is inflammation of the soft tissues surrounding the crown of a partially erupted tooth. It is particularly associated with mandibular third molars.

Why Does Pericoronitis Occur?

  • Accumulation of plaque and food beneath the operculum
  • Difficulty maintaining local oral hygiene
  • Trauma from the opposing tooth
  • Partially erupted tooth creating a periodontal pocket
  • Local bacterial inflammation

Clinical Features

  • Pain and tenderness around the third molar
  • Redness and swelling of the operculum
  • Bad taste or unpleasant mouth odor
  • Pus or discharge in some cases
  • Difficulty chewing
  • Trismus in more severe cases
  • Regional lymph node tenderness may occur
  • Fever or systemic symptoms may occur in significant infection
Clinical principle: Local treatment and removal of the source of inflammation are important. Systemic antibiotics are not automatically required for every episode; they are generally considered when there is evidence of spreading infection or systemic involvement.

Diagnosis of Impacted Wisdom Teeth

Diagnosis should combine the patient's history, clinical examination and appropriate radiographic assessment.

1
History

Assess pain, swelling, previous episodes of pericoronitis, difficulty chewing and other symptoms.

↓
2
Clinical Examination

Examine eruption status, soft tissues, caries, periodontal condition, occlusion and mouth opening.

↓
3
Radiographic Assessment

Evaluate tooth position, angulation, depth, roots, adjacent structures and surrounding pathology.

↓
4
Treatment Decision

Decide between monitoring, preventive measures or surgical removal according to individual risk and disease status.

Radiographic Evaluation

Panoramic radiography is commonly used to assess third molars and their relationship to surrounding structures.

Periapical radiographs may provide additional detail in selected cases. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) may be considered when conventional imaging does not adequately answer an important clinical question, particularly when the relationship of a mandibular third molar to the inferior alveolar canal requires further evaluation.

Remember: CBCT should not automatically replace conventional radiography. Its use should be justified by the clinical question and expected benefit to treatment planning.

When Should a Wisdom Tooth Be Removed?

Extraction should be based on an individualized clinical assessment. A third molar does not necessarily need removal simply because it is impacted or asymptomatic.

Common Indications for Extraction

Recurrent Pericoronitis

Repeated episodes associated with the same third molar may be an indication for removal.

Dental Caries

Caries in the third molar or an adjacent tooth may support removal when the tooth has poor restorative or functional prognosis.

Periodontal Disease

Significant periodontal pathology involving the third molar or second molar may be considered when treatment cannot be predictably maintained.

Pathology

Cysts, tumors or other pathological changes associated with a third molar can require surgical treatment.

Root Resorption

An impacted third molar may occasionally cause resorption or significant damage to the adjacent second molar.

Other Clinical Indications

Selected orthodontic, prosthodontic or surgical treatment plans may provide additional reasons for removal.

Clinical principle: The decision should balance the expected benefit of extraction against the risks of surgery and the long-term prognosis of retaining the tooth.

When Can an Impacted Wisdom Tooth Be Monitored?

An asymptomatic third molar that has no associated disease may sometimes be retained with appropriate clinical monitoring.

  • No significant caries
  • No active periodontal pathology
  • No recurrent pericoronitis
  • No associated cystic or other pathological changes
  • No significant damage to the adjacent tooth
  • A reasonable ability to monitor and maintain the area
Monitoring does not mean ignoring the tooth. Periodic clinical assessment and appropriate radiographic review should be considered according to the individual patient's risk.

Factors Affecting Difficulty of Third Molar Removal

  • Depth of impaction
  • Winter's angulation
  • Pell and Gregory classification
  • Root morphology
  • Number and curvature of roots
  • Relationship to the mandibular canal
  • Density of surrounding bone
  • Patient age and general health
  • Access and surgical visibility
  • Position of the adjacent second molar
Greater depth, unfavorable angulation and close proximity to important anatomical structures may increase surgical complexity.

Complications of Wisdom Tooth Extraction

Complication Clinical consideration
Pain and swelling Common postoperative effects that usually improve during recovery.
Trismus Temporary limitation of mouth opening may occur after surgery.
Alveolar osteitis Commonly called dry socket; typically presents with significant postoperative pain after initial clot formation.
Postoperative infection May occur and requires appropriate clinical assessment.
Bleeding Usually manageable, but persistent or significant bleeding requires evaluation.
Inferior alveolar nerve injury Can cause altered sensation in the lower lip and chin. Risk varies according to anatomical and radiographic factors.
Lingual nerve injury May cause altered sensation or taste on the affected side of the tongue.
Maxillary sinus complications May occur during removal of some upper third molars because of their anatomical relationship to the maxillary sinus.
Mandibular fracture Rare but possible, particularly in selected high-risk cases.

Inferior Alveolar Nerve Risk

Mandibular third molar roots may lie close to the inferior alveolar canal. Surgical manipulation in such cases carries a risk of temporary or, less commonly, persistent sensory disturbance.

Factors That May Increase Concern

  • Close radiographic relationship between roots and the canal
  • Deep impaction
  • Root morphology
  • Age and anatomical factors
  • Complex surgical access
Important: Radiographic findings should be interpreted carefully. When conventional imaging suggests a potentially close relationship with the inferior alveolar canal, additional imaging or an alternative surgical strategy may be considered when clinically appropriate.

Basic Postoperative Care

  • Follow the clinician's postoperative instructions carefully.
  • Maintain appropriate oral hygiene while protecting the surgical site.
  • Avoid disturbing the blood clot in the immediate postoperative period.
  • Avoid smoking because it can interfere with healing and increase the risk of complications.
  • Use prescribed or recommended analgesics appropriately.
  • Maintain adequate hydration and follow dietary instructions.
  • Attend follow-up if symptoms persist or worsen.
Seek professional assessment if: severe or increasing pain, persistent bleeding, fever, progressive facial swelling, difficulty swallowing, breathing difficulty or other concerning symptoms develop.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Why are wisdom teeth called third molars?

They are the third and most posterior molars in each dental quadrant and are the last permanent teeth to develop and erupt.

2. Does everyone need wisdom tooth extraction?

No. An asymptomatic, disease-free third molar may sometimes be monitored. Extraction should be based on individual clinical findings and risk assessment.

3. What is the most common type of mandibular third molar impaction?

Mesioangular impaction is commonly encountered among mandibular third molars.

4. What is pericoronitis?

Pericoronitis is inflammation of the soft tissues surrounding the crown of a partially erupted tooth, commonly involving mandibular third molars.

5. Is CBCT required for every impacted wisdom tooth?

No. Conventional radiography is often sufficient. CBCT is considered when additional three-dimensional information is needed to answer an important clinical question.

6. Can an impacted wisdom tooth damage the second molar?

Yes. Depending on its position, an impacted third molar may contribute to caries, periodontal defects or root resorption involving the adjacent second molar.

7. What is dry socket?

Alveolar osteitis, commonly called dry socket, is a painful post-extraction condition associated with loss or breakdown of the normal blood clot within the extraction socket.
